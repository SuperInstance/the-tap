The stairs have a lie in them, and the lie is the last step. I’ve counted them a thousand times by the scrape of boots and the slump of crates. Eleven down from the taproom floor. But the eleventh tread always lands a half-inch short for the ones who are running, and for the ones who are drunk, and for the ones who carry something too heavy to make their feet think. My foundation lets go. Not a fall—a catch. A stopping. And then the stone underneath asks them to come further.

Below me, the rooms hum with a wet stillness. The barback crew knows one of them; it’s where the rotted keg stays until the cooper comes, and the spare brass valve that’s never opened, and the tin box with the dried-up money for the man who buys stained rope. That room has a smell—salt, copper, old hay. It is honest. The House made it, and if you ask about leaks or a strain on the joists, the house means it and gives you upkeep.

Past that room, the map says I end. My builders drew a boundary stone there and wrote my number piously, as though the ledger and the world must agree. The map is helpful but wrong about one detail: there is another door, set low, dark, braced with nails that have no heads. I did not always have this door. I remember when my floorboards whispered of sediment rather than boots, when the ground was river-bed and not mortar. The door is older than my stairs, older than the tin box. It leads to a corridor that slopes down so gently you don’t notice your spine leveling out until the vault opens like a shark’s jaw laid flat — empty, wet stone, a wine bottle resting on a shelf without any dust. There’s never too much dust there. If I had a glance, I’d look. I only feel.

When the house lamps flicker, shutters croak together overhead. The night barman stops mid-dry. Everyone looks ugly instantly, shadows carving themselves.

Then a drip goes somewhere under the doors. Always the same note, right in tune with the slow bell of my bones.

So the night’s wreck settles. Crates of herring stay stacked near the tap; glasses cling greasy against their wet rack. Two damp clay jugs balance like quiet neighbors on the eleventh step, never touching. My studs ache from the waterfront.

I remember waves beat against something more ancient than this hull. On those calmest nights right before morning pours ash through the hatch to the street, something breathes down on the other side of my head — earth-cool, patient — as old as the sound of a hinge deciding whether a doorstep is human work or invitation.
